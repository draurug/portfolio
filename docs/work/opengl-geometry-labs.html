OpenGL geometry labs

Five labs from a university graphics course, seven C++ programs in total, since lab 3 splits into 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3. Labs 1 to 3 cover context setup, transforms and animation on the fixed pipeline. Lab 4 decides segment intersection with an orientation predicate and rasterizes the segments with Bresenham and with Xiaolin Wu. Lab 5 builds a convex hull by Graham scan and triangulates a polygon by ear clipping.

Labs 1 to 3

Lab 1 is the bootstrap: glutInit, a single-buffered RGB window, glewInit with its return value checked, and one GL_TRIANGLES block whose three vertices carry three different colors. The fixed pipeline interpolates the colors across the face, so the triangle comes out Gouraud-shaded with no extra code. Forty lines in total. It confirms that the toolchain links and the context comes up.

Lab 2 adds a modelview matrix and keyboard input. Arrow keys translate a triangle, +/- scale, q/e rotate, r resets. The transforms are issued as glTranslatef · glRotatef · glScalef inside a glPushMatrix/glPopMatrix pair, in that order. That order keeps rotation and scaling centred on the figure rather than on the world origin. Swapping the first two calls is the usual way to get this wrong.

Lab 3 is three variants of one animation, written as three separate programs. The figure is the parametric heart x = 16sin³t, y = 13cos t − 5cos 2t − 2cos 3t − cos 4t, sampled at one-degree steps into a GL_TRIANGLE_FAN. In 3.1 the figure travels an ellipse. In 3.2 it spins in place. In 3.3 it does both, with space to pause and c to cycle colors. A 16 ms glutTimerFunc drives the phase, which ties animation speed to the timer rather than to frame rate.

Lab 4: segment intersection

Lab 4 takes two line segments and reports whether they meet. The program never solves for the crossing point. Computing that point would require a division, a guard for the parallel case and a loss of precision, and the predicate avoids all three. Everything rests on one primitive:

int orientation(Point p, Point q, Point r)
{
    float v = (q.y - p.y) * (r.x - q.x) - (q.x - p.x) * (r.y - q.y);
    if (std::abs(v) < 0.00001f) return 0;
    return v > 0 ? 1 : 2;
}

That expression is a 2×2 determinant, the z-component of the cross product of q−p and r−q. Its magnitude is twice the area of triangle pqr. Only the sign is used, and the sign says which side of the directed line pq the point r falls on. Zero means collinear.

Intersection follows from a straddle argument. In general position, p₁q₁ and p₂q₂ cross exactly when p₂ and q₂ lie on opposite sides of line p₁q₁ and p₁ and q₁ lie on opposite sides of line p₂q₂. One condition alone is insufficient, since a short segment can straddle an infinite line while sitting far off the end of the actual segment. Both conditions together pin the crossing inside both segments. In code that is four orientation calls and the test o1 != o2 && o3 != o4.

The four collinear cases are handled separately. When an orientation returns 0 the segments share a line, and the question collapses to whether a point lies between two others on that line. onSegment answers with a bounding-box comparison, which would be wrong as a general containment test and is exact under a proven collinearity.

The whole predicate is a handful of multiplications and comparisons: constant time, no division, no special case for vertical or parallel segments.

On floating-point input the 1e-5 epsilon does real work. It decides what counts as collinear, and near-degenerate cases land on whichever side of it the arithmetic falls. With integer coordinates the determinant is exact and the epsilon disappears. That is the version worth shipping.

Bresenham and Wu

The same two segment pairs are rasterized twice, once per column of the window, so the two algorithms run on identical input. Intersecting pairs are drawn green and disjoint pairs red, which puts the predicate's answer on screen instead of in a print statement.

Bresenham keeps an integer error term err = dx - dy and at each step decides from its sign whether to advance x, y, or both. No floating point appears anywhere: the loop is additions, subtractions and comparisons on integers. It emits exactly one fully-lit pixel per step, so the line is crisp and the staircase is visible.

Wu answers a different question: how much of each pixel the line covers. It walks the major axis keeping a floating-point interY, advances it by gradient = dy/dx each column, and lights the two pixels bracketing it with brightnesses rfpart(interY) and fpart(interY). Those two sum to 1, so intensity per column is constant. Endpoints get an xGap factor so that a line starting mid-pixel does not begin with a full-brightness stub. Steep lines are transposed up front, which leaves the loop handling only the shallow case.

Wu costs twice the pixel writes, floating-point instead of integer arithmetic, and a blend-enabled framebuffer (GL_SRC_ALPHA, GL_ONE_MINUS_SRC_ALPHA). Its correctness also depends on what is already under the line, because partial pixels composite with the background. Both algorithms are O(max(dx, dy)). The difference is in the constant factor.

One flaw: putPixel wraps every single pixel in its own glBegin/glEnd pair. That is one draw call per pixel. At a few thousand pixels in a static scene the cost is invisible, and it would be indefensible in any real renderer.

Lab 5: convex hull and triangulation

Two panels share one window, split with glViewport. Thirty random points with their hull sit on the left, a random simple polygon cut into triangles on the right. R regenerates both, so every run is a fresh test case rather than the one input that happened to work.

Graham scan

The pivot is the point with lowest y, leftmost on ties, and is guaranteed to lie on the hull. The remaining points are sorted by polar angle about it, using the cross-product sign as the comparator rather than atan2, with ties broken by squared distance. The sorted list is then walked with a stack, popping before each push while the last two entries and the candidate do not make a left turn:

while (res.size() >= 2 &&
       cross(res[res.size() - 2], res[res.size() - 1], pts[i]) <= 0.0f)
    res.pop_back();

The <= 0 rather than < 0 also discards collinear points, so the hull comes back with corners only and no redundant vertices along an edge. Complexity is O(n log n), dominated by the sort: each point is pushed once and popped at most once, so the scan itself is amortized O(n).

Ear clipping

The polygon is first normalized to counter-clockwise orientation using the shoelace signed area, since every subsequent sign test assumes that convention. Vertices are then removed one at a time, each removal emitting a triangle.

A vertex is an ear when two conditions hold. First, it is convex: cross(prev, curr, next) > 0 in a counter-clockwise polygon, meaning the triangle cut off there points into the polygon. Second, the triangle (prev, curr, next) is empty, with no other vertex inside it. The second condition is not redundant, because a convex vertex whose triangle swallows a distant vertex would produce a triangle sticking outside the shape. isEar therefore tests every remaining vertex against the candidate triangle with three cross-product signs.

An ear always exists by the two-ears theorem: every simple polygon with more than three vertices has at least two. The loop therefore terminates, removing one vertex per pass until three remain, which gives n − 2 triangles for an n-gon. Cost is O(n) per ear test and O(n) tests per removal over O(n) removals, so O(n³) in the naive form written here. For 7 to 10 vertex polygons that is irrelevant. For a mesh it would not be, and the standard fix is to cache convexity and revalidate only the two neighbours of a removed ear.

The code also carries a guard counter and an if (!found) break;. Neither should fire on a simple polygon. They exist because the random generator could in principle produce a self-intersecting one, and a lab that hangs inside glutMainLoop is harder to debug than one that draws an incomplete triangulation.

This is coursework: fixed-function OpenGL, single-file programs, no build system beyond a compiler invocation. The algorithms are textbook ones implemented from the definitions rather than called from a library, which was the point of the exercise.
