Bin packing and the blockchain/AI intersection

Two expository essays written for a foundations-of-blockchain course. The first works through the NP-completeness proof for bin packing and applies it to block construction. The second surveys where blockchain and machine learning reinforce each other. Neither is original research.

Both papers are coursework and contain no new results. The bin packing essay reproduces a standard reduction and a set of known approximation bounds, then argues that those bounds describe something blockchain clients already do. The AI essay is a survey that organises existing projects and proposals around one claim about where the two technologies fit together. The bin packing essay treats "the miner builds a block" as a combinatorial optimisation problem with a known hardness result, which is an unusual framing for that operation.

Bin packing as an NP-complete problem

This is the longer of the two essays. It opens with the complexity-theory machinery it needs: deterministic and nondeterministic Turing machines, the classes P and NP, polynomial-time reduction, and the distinction between NP-hard and NP-complete. It then applies the standard two-part scheme to bin packing.

The problem: given items of sizes s_1, ..., s_n and bins of fixed capacity C, pack every item so that no bin's contents exceed C, using as few bins as possible. The decision version asks whether K bins suffice, and the proof addresses that version.

Membership in NP is the easy half. A certificate is an assignment of each item to a bin index, which is O(n log K) long. A verifier sums the sizes per bin and compares against C, costing O(n) additions and K comparisons, so verification runs in polynomial time.

NP-hardness comes from a reduction from PARTITION, which splits a multiset of positive integers with sum 2T into two parts of sum T each. The reduction is about as small as reductions get: take the items to be the numbers themselves, set the bin capacity to T, and ask for K = 2 bins. The forward direction is immediate. In the reverse direction, two bins of capacity T hold at most 2T in total and the items sum to exactly 2T, so both capacity inequalities are forced to be equalities, each bin is loaded to exactly T, and the two bins give the partition directly. There is no slack anywhere in the argument.

Inapproximability

The same reduction does more work than the NP-completeness proof needs. In the constructed instance, a packing into 2 bins exists precisely when the numbers split evenly. Any polynomial-time algorithm that could reliably tell "optimum = 2" from "optimum = 3" would therefore decide PARTITION. That rules out an approximation ratio better than 3/2 in the absolute worst case, assuming P ≠ NP.

The 3/2 barrier is an absolute worst-case statement. It says nothing about the asymptotic regime, and that distinction is what makes the problem tractable in practice.

Once the optimum is large, the picture changes. An asymptotic PTAS exists (Fernandez de la Vega and Lueker, 1981), and the Karmarkar–Karp algorithm (1982) uses OPT + O(log² OPT) bins, an additive overhead that vanishes relative to the optimum. The problem is thus hard to approximate well in the worst case and easy to approximate well asymptotically, and both facts follow from the same small reduction.

Heuristics and their guarantees

The essay collects the classical bounds, which are the ones used in practice:

The online/offline split carries the practical weight. First Fit Decreasing gets its better constant by sorting items largest-first, which requires knowing all the items up front. When items arrive one at a time and placement is irrevocable, only Next Fit, First Fit and Best Fit apply, with their weaker bounds. For exact solutions the essay points at integer linear programming and, as the strongest current approach, column generation over the Gilmore–Gomory formulation.

Application to blockchain

The connecting argument is that a blockchain is full of fixed-capacity containers being filled with discrete units. A block has a hard ceiling, block weight in Bitcoin and the gas limit in Ethereum, and transactions have sizes. The essay separates two problems that often get conflated: maximising total fee within a single block is knapsack, while distributing a whole pending pool across the minimum number of blocks is bin packing.

The cleanest instance is rollup batching. Since EIP-4844, L2 data is posted in fixed-size blobs of roughly 128 KB, and each blob is paid for separately. Packing compressed transactions into the fewest blobs is bin packing with no reinterpretation needed, and the objective function is a cost in money. Sharding and data-availability storage get shorter treatments as further variants. In practice, block builders run an online greedy pass, ordering the mempool by fee per unit of gas and filling while space remains. That blends the knapsack objective with bin packing's capacity constraint and sits in the weaker online regime by necessity.

Blockchain and artificial intelligence

The second essay is a survey. Its organising claim is that the two technologies have complementary failure modes: AI is centralised, opaque and dependent on trusting its training data, while a blockchain is decentralised and auditable but has no analytical capability and is slow and expensive to compute on.

The essay works through that claim in both directions. Blockchain contributes to AI mainly by making claims about AI verifiable rather than by performing computation: data provenance that records what a model was trained on, immutable decision logs for auditability in regulated settings, token incentives for contributing data or compute, and coordination for federated learning without exposing private data. zkML is the one mechanism here that gives a cryptographic guarantee rather than an audit trail. It uses zero-knowledge proofs to establish that a specific model processed specific inputs and produced a specific output, without revealing weights or data.

In the other direction, AI is applied to blockchain data and code: on-chain analytics for fraud and AML detection, static analysis of contracts for reentrancy and access-control bugs before deployment, fee forecasting, and filtering for oracle inputs. The survey then covers decentralised AI, agents holding their own wallets, DePIN compute markets and content provenance, with Ocean Protocol, Bittensor, SingularityNET, Fetch.ai and Numerai as the worked examples.

The limitations section is the part I would defend most readily. Full on-chain inference remains far too expensive. The transparency a blockchain provides is in direct tension with the confidentiality most training data requires, and the standards involved are early. The conclusion is that this is a direction rather than a solved problem.
